01The challenge

A retailer running on spreadsheets

Boreal Trail Supply Co. is a fictional outdoor-gear retailer with two stores, a distribution centre and an online store. It has around 48,000 customers and 3,000 products across six categories, and it relies on spreadsheets exported from an aging cash register system.

When we reviewed the legacy dataset, we identified four issues.

Duplicate customer records
Several customers appear more than once under different email addresses, so multiple records exist instead of one consistent profile.
Invalid discount values
Only a few discount values should be valid, yet the file shows discounts greater than 100%.
No enforced rules
A spreadsheet has nothing to force the data to be valid, so errors accumulate as the business grows.
One supplier per product
The legacy file records a single supplier for each product, which cannot represent a product supplied by several suppliers.

We reviewed the file ourselves and used AI to check for irregularities. It flagged the duplicate customer emails and the discount values above 100%, and we verified both in the data before including them in our scope.

Our goal: replace the spreadsheet-based approach with a relational database that enforces its own rules.

Our extension: used-gear consignment and buyback

In addition to selling products, Boreal Trail takes in used outdoor gear to be resold. Each item is graded and inspected before being listed for sale. Once it sells, the consignor receives a payout that depends on the item's grade and on how long it was available for sale (0–30, 31–60 or 61+ days). Any item unsold past 90 days is returned to the consignor or donated, based on the consignor's preference.

02The model

From business rules to an ER model

We wrote 15 business rules, documented our design assumptions wherever the brief was silent, and modelled the result in an entity-relationship diagram (ERD) with 16 entities, including the consignment extension.

The numbered markers on the diagram point to four modelling decisions, described below it.

  1. Locations as one entity

    The website and the warehouse are modelled as location records rather than separate entities, so orders and inventory reuse a single location foreign key.

  2. Product–supplier bridge

    The many-to-many relationship between products and suppliers is resolved by the product_supplier bridge entity, which also carries the supplier SKU and unit cost.

  3. Price at the time of sale

    order_line stores unit_price_at_sale, the price charged when the transaction occurred, separately from the current catalogue price on product.

  4. Payout by grade and day-band

    Consignor payout depends on the item's grade and on the day-band in which it sells (0–30, 31–60, 61+ days). GRADE and DAY_BAND_RATE form a many-to-many relationship, resolved by the grade_day_band_rate bridge entity.

From business rules to the ERD

BR-7Each ORDER belongs to exactly one CUSTOMER; each CUSTOMER may create one or many ORDERs.

customer_order carries customer_id, a foreign key referencing customer.

BR-12Each ORDER must be placed at exactly one LOCATION; one LOCATION can receive many ORDERs.

customer_order carries location_id, so store, web and warehouse orders share one structure.

BR-14Each GRADE has a payout rate for one or more DAY_BAND_RATE; each DAY_BAND_RATE has a payout rate for one or more GRADE.

A many-to-many relationship resolved by grade_day_band_rate, which stores the payout percentage for each grade and day-band pair.

03The build

A normalized schema, built in MySQL

We converted the ER model into a normalized relational schema and implemented it in MySQL with primary keys, foreign keys, composite keys and constraints, then populated it with sample data and tested the script.

The script creates the independent tables first (customer, location, category, supplier), then the tables with single-parent foreign keys (product, inventory, customer_order), then the tables with multiple or composite foreign keys (order_line, product_supplier).

Composite key and constraints in ORDER_LINE

The order_line table combines several of the design tools in one place. The highlighted lines are the keys and constraints.

CREATE TABLE order_line (
    order_id            INT          NOT NULL,
    line_no             SMALLINT     NOT NULL,
    product_id          INT          NOT NULL,
    qty                 INT          NOT NULL,
    unit_price_at_sale  DECIMAL(8,2) NOT NULL,
    discount_pct        DECIMAL(4,2) NOT NULL DEFAULT 0.0,
    PRIMARY KEY (order_id, line_no),
    FOREIGN KEY (order_id)   REFERENCES customer_order(order_id),
    FOREIGN KEY (product_id) REFERENCES product(product_id),
    CHECK (qty > 0),
    CHECK (discount_pct BETWEEN 0 AND 90)
);
  • Composite primary key. A line is identified by order_id and line_no together.
  • Foreign keys. They enforce referential integrity: every line references an existing order and product.
  • CHECK constraints. Quantity must be positive and discount_pct is limited to 0–90, addressing the discounts above 100% found in the legacy data.

Historical pricing: two prices, two facts

product.unit_price is the current catalogue price. order_line.unit_price_at_sale is the price the customer actually paid when the transaction occurred. These are different facts, so we intentionally store both.

Order 10412, 14 March 2025

$329.00

unit_price_at_sale, the price paid

Today

$349.00

unit_price, the current catalogue price of the Nordic 2P Tent

If prices were read from the product table, old receipts, sales reports and refunds would change whenever the catalogue price changed. A refund on this order should return the $329.00 the customer paid, not today's $349.00.

Normalization check. unit_price_at_sale can look like a dependency on product_id, but product_id does not determine it: the Nordic 2P Tent sold for $329.00 on order 10412 while the catalogue lists $349.00. The column depends on the whole composite key, so order_line remains in third normal form (3NF).

PRODUCT_SUPPLIER: resolving a many-to-many relationship

A product can be supplied by several suppliers and a supplier provides many products, so the relationship is resolved with a bridge table keyed on product_id and supplier_id.

CREATE TABLE product_supplier (
    supplier_id   INT          NOT NULL,
    product_id    INT          NOT NULL,
    supplier_sku  VARCHAR(30)  NOT NULL,
    unit_cost     DECIMAL(8,2) NOT NULL,
    PRIMARY KEY (product_id, supplier_id),
    FOREIGN KEY (supplier_id) REFERENCES supplier(supplier_id),
    FOREIGN KEY (product_id)  REFERENCES product(product_id),
    CHECK (unit_cost > 0)
);
  • Composite primary key. It prevents the same product–supplier pair from being recorded twice.
  • Room to grow. Our sample data lists one supplier per product, but the structure supports several.

Testing integrity rules

The script ends with two reject INSERT statements, kept commented out so the script runs cleanly. Each violates a named constraint: a duplicate primary key on customer (entity integrity) and an order line referencing a product that does not exist (referential integrity).

-- Referential-integrity violation: product_id 9999 does not exist in
-- product, so this fails the FOREIGN KEY order_line.product_id ->
-- product.product_id.
INSERT INTO order_line (order_id, line_no, product_id, qty, unit_price_at_sale, discount_pct)
VALUES (10412, 3, 9999, 1, 50.00, 0);
  • With the comment markers removed, this INSERT fails the foreign key from order_line.product_id to product.product_id.

Scope of the build

The ERD covers the full design, including the consignment extension. The MySQL build implements the nine core tables; the extension tables (consigned_item, consignor, grade, day_band_rate and payout) are modelled but not built.

In total, the script defines nine tables, nine foreign keys, five CHECK constraints and three composite primary keys.

04The analysis

From SQL to findings

We used the database to answer managerial questions, moving from SQL to results to findings. We wrote eight queries; a selection follows, each with what the result shows and its limitations.

All figures come from the small sample dataset built for the project: five products, four customers and four orders. They demonstrate the queries, not the performance of a real retailer.

Which five products generate the most revenue?

Result The Nordic 2P Tent generates the most revenue at $329.00, followed by the Laurentide Sandal Pro ($231.47) and the Boreal Down Jacket ($210.81). The five products total $948.77, all of the revenue in the sample. Revenue is calculated from unit_price_at_sale, so the tent is counted at the $329 paid rather than today's $349 catalogue price.

Limitations The sample contains only five products, so the top five is the entire catalogue. The ranking would be more meaningful across the full catalogue of 3,000 products.

Revenue by product

    View the SQL 
    SELECT
    p.product_name,
    SUM(ol.qty * ol.unit_price_at_sale) AS total_revenue
FROM order_line ol
JOIN product p ON ol.product_id = p.product_id
GROUP BY p.product_id, p.product_name
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC
LIMIT 5;

    What is the margin between each product's current selling price and its cheapest supplier cost?

    Result In dollars, the Nordic 2P Tent has the largest margin ($144) and the Trail Stove Mini the smallest ($30). As a share of catalogue price the difference narrows: all five products fall between 40% and 44%.

    Limitations Margin is catalogue price minus supplier unit cost, so it excludes discounts, shipping and store costs. Each product has one supplier in the sample, so the cheapest supplier is simply that supplier.

      05Key takeaways

      Key takeaways

      • Database design

        Business rules and documented assumptions are the foundation of the schema. Where the brief was silent, writing our assumptions down made each modelling decision explicit.

      • Data integrity

        Constraints enforce integrity in the database itself. Primary keys, foreign keys and CHECK rules reject invalid data, such as a discount above 100%, before it is stored.

      • SQL

        Joins determine which rows appear in a result. An inner join removed an order with no line items from two of our outputs, so we noted it alongside each result.

      • Business analysis

        A figure needs context. The tent earns the most dollars per unit, yet as a share of catalogue price the five products fall within four percentage points of each other.

      • Validating conclusions

        A correct query can still mislead. Before stating a finding, we checked what each result leaves out: small samples, excluded rows and costs the calculation ignores.

      • Verifying AI output

        AI is a reviewer, not the author. We tested each suggestion against source data, the project brief, course material or MySQL documentation before accepting, correcting or rejecting it.
